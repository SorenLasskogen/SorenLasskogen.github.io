Walking on Water

An audiovisual floor you can walk on: a bubble of light follows you, and your position and voice shape the water and the sound around you.

  • Python
  • YOLO11
  • OpenCV
  • TouchDesigner
  • Ableton Live
  • OSC
Context
Exam project, AHCI course, Medialogy
Timeline
Autumn 2025
Team
3 people
Built with
Python, Ultralytics YOLO11, ByteTrack, OpenCV, python-osc, TouchDesigner, Ableton Live, Max for Live
Hardware
2 mini projectors, ceiling webcam, SM58 microphone, studio monitors

The problem

After building a framework for interactive installations the semester before, we wanted to try a full-body experience. The goal was a multimodal system with two inputs — your position and your voice — and two outputs — a projection and a soundscape — inspired by works like teamLab’s interactive rooms.

My role

One of three in the group, building a system that spans computer vision, real-time visuals and live sound.

How we built it

Tracking

A webcam mounted on a laser-cut bracket in the ceiling looks straight down, so no 3D-to-2D conversion is needed. A Python program runs Ultralytics YOLO11 (the nano model, for frame rate) with ByteTrack to follow people between frames. It takes the centre of each bounding box, rescales it for each program — −1 to 1 for TouchDesigner, 1 to 100 for Ableton Live — and sends it as OSC messages over a local hotspot to two computers.

Visuals

In TouchDesigner, a bubble follows the user across the floor. The microphone level, smoothed with a one-euro filter, drives the bubble’s size; past a threshold it sends out a pulse. Noise operators modulated by LFOs, then displaced, edge-detected and bloomed, give the water its look. Two overlapping projectors fill in each other’s shadows.

Sound

In Ableton Live, a Max for Live OSC receiver maps the user’s position onto two XY pads over an underwater recording: an EQ (x = filter frequency, y = resonance) and a tremolo in ring-modulator mode (x = coarse, y = LFO rate).

Outcome

The installation worked as intended, but exposed a classic trade-off: the small projectors needed darkness, while the camera needed light to track reliably. Tracker ID switches also meant scaling down from three simultaneous users to one.

A second version moved the projection to a wall and tracked hands instead of bodies. With a brighter projector and well-lit participants, it performed far better and gave more parameters to play with. Next steps we identified: a depth or lidar camera, running the tracking inside TouchDesigner to cut latency, and individual lavalier mics so each person gets their own voice-driven bubble.
