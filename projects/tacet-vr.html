TACET VR

Turning a linear short film into an interactive VR experience — and testing whether it changes how audiences understand the story.

  • Unity
  • C#
  • Meta Quest 3
  • XR Interaction Toolkit
  • FMOD
  • Blender

The problem

Film gives a director tight control over what the audience sees. VR hands that control to the viewer, who can look anywhere and move freely. TACET is a short film about Adam, caught in a car full of peers whose tense hierarchy weighs on him, while visions of his boss, his mother and an oracle-like figure guide him towards the end of his suffering.

We asked: can a linear film be extended into an interactive VR experience — and does that bring the audience’s interpretation closer to the director’s intent, or dilute it?

How we built it

We worked from the director’s guiding document, a moodboard and a storyboard to design interactive narrative beats that extend the film rather than retell it. TACET VR runs natively on Meta Quest 3 with six degrees of freedom, built in Unity with the XR Interaction Toolkit and a custom sequencing system that plays the narrative beats in order.

  • Guiding attention. With no camera to frame shots, we used lighting, spatial audio cues and environmental storytelling — objects and spaces that tell the story without dialogue.
  • Custom assets. Props and animations were modelled in Blender and textured in Substance Painter to match the film’s tone.
  • Spatial audio. Sound was built in FMOD, with the Meta XR Audio SDK handling HRTF spatialisation.
  • Performance and comfort. Running standalone on a headset meant strict budgets — static geometry, simple URP shaders and requirements to limit cybersickness.

Outcome

After usability testing with SUS and VRSUQ, we ran an A/B study: one group experienced TACET VR, the other watched the story linearly. We used qualitative interviews and the Igroup Presence Questionnaire (IPQ).

VR did not significantly improve how accurately people interpreted the narrative — but it clearly changed how they engaged with it. VR participants described the story from an embodied, first-person perspective, while linear viewers stayed observers. High presence scores suggest interactive VR can change how audiences engage with a story while preserving the director’s message.
